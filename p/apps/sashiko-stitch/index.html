Description
Sashiko began centuries ago in the cold farming and fishing villages of northern Japan as a practical answer to a hard problem: cotton was scarce and imported, so families reinforced and patched worn hemp work clothes with rows of running stitch in white thread, and over generations those repairs became deliberate geometric designs. Sashiko Stitch turns "I want to try sashiko" into a real, matched plan. Describe your project - a small hole in an old shirt sleeve, a plain tote bag you want to decorate, a jacket collar getting thin - and how much hand-stitching experience you have, and our AI matches you to one of three real regional techniques: moyo-zashi, pattern stitching worked in a continuous running line to trace motifs like asanoha (hemp leaf) or shippou (interlocking circles), suited to visible decorative mending on almost any fabric; hitomezashi, a single-stitch grid technique from the Shonai region of Yamagata Prefecture built by counting threads on an even grid to form patterns like kaki-no-hana (persimmon flower), well suited to reinforcing a worn area evenly; or kogin-zashi, an odd-count counted-thread technique from the Tsugaru region of Aomori Prefecture, historically stitched by farming families onto coarse hemp work clothing in dense diamond-based motifs to add both warmth and strength. Every match comes with a real stitch-by-stitch walkthrough: how to mark your grid or motif, what thread and needle weight the technique traditionally uses, how many strands to work with, and the pacing that keeps the stitch spacing even. The Pattern Library tab lets you browse all three techniques and their named motifs as a standalone reference at any time, even without running a match.

Why This App

Sashiko tutorials online are usually either a single pattern shared out of context or a dense historical essay with no clear starting stitch, leaving a beginner unsure which of the several named techniques actually fits their project. Sashiko Stitch starts from something anyone can describe in a sentence - what they're mending or making and how confident they are with a needle - and turns it into a real matched technique and the exact stitch sequence to follow, so a craft that can feel intimidating on paper becomes an approachable first project.
