Description
Mizuhiki - the stiff, colored paper cords tied around Japanese gift envelopes and packages - usually reads as decorative string to anyone outside Japan. What's missing is that the knot itself is a message: which knot is tied, in which colors, with how many cords, tells the recipient exactly what kind of occasion this is and whether it's meant to happen again. Mizuhiki Knot closes that gap. Describe an occasion in a sentence, and our AI, working from documented mizuhiki etiquette, matches it to one of three real knot styles - Musubikiri, the tight binding knot that cannot be re-tied once pulled closed, used for weddings, funerals, and get-well gifts because the wish embedded in it is "may this not happen again"; Chomusubi, the butterfly bow knot that can be untied and re-tied, used for births, graduations, and business openings because these are occasions people hope will happily recur; and Awaji-musubi, the interlocking knot whose looped ends can be pulled to tighten without ever coming fully undone, used for New Year gifts and weddings alike to symbolize a bond that lasts. Each match also explains the color and strand-count conventions layered on top of the knot: red-and-white cords for celebrations against black-and-white or navy-and-white for funerals and condolences, gold-and-silver reserved for the most formal weddings, and the strand count itself - three cords for a simple gift, five as the standard baseline of courtesy, seven for deeper respect, and ten (two bundles of five, one for each partner) reserved for weddings as a doubly auspicious number. Odd counts are used for celebrations because a number that cannot be evenly divided symbolizes a bond that cannot be broken. The app also covers the history behind the custom: mizuhiki is traced to 607 CE, when the envoy Ono no Imoko returned from the Sui court in China with gifts bound in red-and-white hemp cords, a practice the imperial court then adopted for its own offerings. By the Muromachi period (1333-1568) the cords had shifted from hemp to twisted, glue-stiffened washi paper - the technique still used today - and the custom spread from the aristocracy to the general public over the course of the Edo period. The Knot Guide tab lets you browse all three knot styles, their colors, and their etiquette rules as a standalone reference at any time, and your past matches are saved to a personal Etiquette Journal.

Why This App

Mizuhiki is sold abroad as pretty gift string, almost never explained as an etiquette system where the wrong knot on the wrong envelope reads as a real social mistake. Mizuhiki Knot starts from something anyone can describe in a sentence - an occasion - and turns it into the correct knot, the correct colors, and an honest explanation of the etiquette behind them, turning decoration into something worth actually understanding.
