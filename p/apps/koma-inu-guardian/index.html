Description
Almost every visitor to a Japanese shrine walks straight past a genuine piece of symbolism: the two stone or bronze lion-dogs flanking the gate. Most photos catch only that one has its mouth open and the other closed, with no idea that this pairing - a-un - is a real concept carried through centuries of religious art, or that the statues themselves come from documented regional carving traditions, each with its own posture and character. Koma-inu Guardian closes that gap. Describe a worry, a goal, or a situation you're facing, and our AI, working from documented shrine-guardian history, matches you to one of six real regional koma-inu carving styles, explains why that style's posture and expression fit what you described, and tells you whether your matched guardian is the a (open-mouth) or un (closed-mouth) half of the pair - and what that actually means. Every match draws from real carving lineages: the Izumo-gamae style from Shimane, considered among the oldest surviving forms, low and crouching with a watchful, coiled-energy posture suited to quiet vigilance; the Naniwa style from Osaka, thickset and heavy-maned, associated with steady endurance; the Okazaki style from Aichi, the sharp-featured, upright form mass-produced from the Meiji era onward and now the most common sight at shrines nationwide, fitting momentum and forward motion; the Edo style, refined and formally posed in old Tokyo tradition, suited to composure under pressure; the Onomichi style from Hiroshima, weathered and characterful from centuries by the sea, fitting resilience through change; and the Sanuki style from Kagawa, playful and rounder-featured, suited to lighter-hearted new beginnings. Every match also explains the a-un pairing itself: the open-mouthed a-gyo figure represents the first sound, birth, and beginning, while the closed-mouthed un-gyo figure represents the last sound, completion, and ending - a real concept borrowed from Sanskrit and Buddhist symbolism, applied here to guardian statues across Japan. The Guardians tab lets you browse all six carving styles as a standalone reference at any time, even without running a match. Your matches are saved to a personal history so you can revisit which guardian stood watch over you on a given day.

Why This App

Koma-inu are treated as photo backdrops, but they carry a documented regional carving history and a real paired symbolism that almost no visitor learns on the spot. Koma-inu Guardian starts from something anyone can describe in a sentence - what's on their mind right now - and turns it into a real matched carving style, a real open-or-closed-mouth meaning, and an honest explanation of the tradition behind both, turning a familiar shrine photo into a small, accurate piece of history.
