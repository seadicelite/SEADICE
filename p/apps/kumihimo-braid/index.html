Description
Kumihimo - the braided silk cords used for centuries on samurai armor, sword mountings, and kimono sashes - are often reduced abroad to "that braided bracelet technique." What rarely travels with the craft is that each structural pattern has a real name and history, that two living regional schools (Iga and Kyo) developed distinct approaches to it, and that the color combinations were never arbitrary - they followed a documented symbolic system. Kumihimo Braid closes that gap. Describe what you're marking - a graduation gift, a wedding accessory, a farewell gift for a retiring mentor, a cord for your own everyday wear - and our AI, working from documented kumihimo history, matches you to one of six real named braid structures, explains which of the two living regional schools it belongs to, and recommends a color combination with its genuine symbolic meaning. Every match draws from real structures: Kongou-gumi, a dense round braid historically used for sword cords (sageo) and armor lacing, valued for strength and durability; Karakumi, a round pattern derived from techniques that reached Japan via Tang-dynasty China, traditionally used for tea-ceremony and formal accessories; Kikkou-gumi, a hexagonal "tortoise-shell" structure carrying a longevity motif; Bishamon-gumi, a square braid named for the guardian deity Bishamonten and associated with protection; Yotsudai-gumi, a flat four-strand braid valued for being simple and highly durable; and Edo Yatsuse-gumi, a flat, wide structure associated with Edo-period obijime sashes. Each match is also placed in one of the two real living regional schools: Iga-Kumihimo, from Mie Prefecture, a government-recognized traditional craft historically tied to tea-ceremony and martial accessories and known for dense, sturdy structures; and Kyo-Kumihimo, from Kyoto, historically favored by court nobility and known for refined, decorative obijime work. Color guidance follows the traditional five-element (gogyo) color symbolism actually used in kumihimo design - for example murasaki (purple) for dignity, aka (red) for vitality and protection, and ai (indigo) for calm composure. The Braids tab lets you browse all six structures and both regional schools as a standalone reference at any time, even without running a match. Your matches are saved to a personal history so you can revisit which braid you chose for a given gift or moment.

Why This App

Kumihimo is usually encountered abroad as a simplified bracelet-braiding trend, stripped of the structural names, regional schools, and color symbolism that make it a documented craft tradition. Kumihimo Braid starts from something anyone can describe in a sentence - who the braid is for and why - and turns it into a real matched structure, a real regional lineage, and an honest explanation of the color meaning behind it, turning a passing craft trend into a small, accurate piece of textile history.
